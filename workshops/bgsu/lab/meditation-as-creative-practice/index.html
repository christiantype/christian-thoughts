Meditation as Creative Practice

Can a sustained, structured practice of attention be taught as a design discipline rather than offered as relief from one? The design industry treats attention as a soft skill — a wellness benefit, a thing you do after the work. Everything a designer produces is downstream of what they were able to notice, which makes attention the first act of the work, not a recovery from it.

Looking

Written after the first year of weekly sittings.

The attendance pattern was the first surprise and remains the most useful finding. The room does not fill and then stabilise; it runs between two and nine people with no discernible relationship to promotion, weather or season. Weeks when it was announced widely were not better attended than weeks when it was not. The practical consequence is that promoting it is close to wasted effort, and the thing that keeps it alive is that it is never canceled — a sitting with two people is a sitting, and moving or skipping it once costs several weeks of trust.

James’s framing — that the skill is return rather than sustained focus — turned out to be the only instruction worth repeating, and it is the one that non-meditators accept without resistance. Framing the hour as “you will get distracted; the practice is coming back” removed nearly all of the initial self-consciousness in the room. Framing it in any vocabulary borrowed from wellness or productivity had the opposite effect and was quietly abandoned.

What did not work: every attempt to connect the sitting to the studio’s output explicitly. A period of running the sitting before a working session, on the theory that it would sharpen the work, produced nothing measurable and made the sitting instrumental — people arrived with an agenda for it, which is precisely the seizing Weil warns against. The sitting works better as infrastructure with no assigned purpose. This is difficult to write on a page that is arguing for its value as a creative practice, and it is the honest result.

Also unresolved: whether any of this is visible in the work. A year in, the claim in the position — that the change would be legible in the artifacts rather than only in the person — is not supported by anything I could show a skeptic. The change I can evidence is in duration: projects are looked at for longer before a direction is chosen, and directions are abandoned later and less dramatically. Whether that constitutes better design or merely slower design is not something a year of data can settle, and the pillar should not claim it can.

Thinking

This is where we test our thinking.

Simon8e Weil states the case in a single sentence, and almost everything written since is commentary on it: “Attention is the rarest and purest form of generosity.” The essay it sits near — “Reflections on the Right Use of School Studies with a View to the Love of God,” collected in Waiting for God9 (1951) — makes an argument that ought to be uncomfortable to anyone who has ever defended a studio practice on the grounds of craft. Weil claims that the content of study is nearly irrelevant. A student doing geometry badly is still developing the capacity that matters, which is the ability to hold something in view without seizing it. Attention, for Weil, is emphatically not effort. It is the opposite of straining: “the soul empties itself of all its own contents in order to receive into itself the being it is looking at, just as he is, in all his truth.”

William James had already given this a psychological body sixty years earlier. In The Principles of Psychology3 (1890) he writes that “the faculty of voluntarily bringing back a wandering attention, over and over again, is the very root of judgment, character, and will,” and immediately adds the line that makes it a pedagogy rather than a platitude — that an education which improved this faculty would be “the education par excellence.” James’s phrasing matters for a design studio: the skill is not sustained attention. It is return. The mind wanders; the practice is the coming back. That reframing is what makes the claim testable. Nobody can be taught not to be distracted. Anybody can be taught to notice that they have been and to come back without a fuss about it, and that is a trainable motor skill with a measurable rep count.

Iris Murdoch carried Weil’s term into moral philosophy and, without intending to, into aesthetics. In The Sovereignty of Good4 (1970) she borrows “attention” explicitly to name “a just and loving gaze directed upon an individual reality,” and argues that moral work happens in the quality of looking that precedes a decision, not in the decision itself. By the time you are choosing, she says, most of the work is already done — the choosing is determined by what you have allowed yourself to see. Replace the moral instance with a design one and the claim survives intact. By the time a designer is selecting a typeface, the consequential work has already happened in how long and how honestly they looked at the problem. Craft is what you do with what you noticed; almost nothing in design education addresses the noticing.

Two artists made this operational, and both of them are more useful to a studio than any of the philosophers. John Cage2’s 4′33″ (1952) is usually explained as a piece about silence and is more accurately a piece about framing: it takes ordinary attention and puts a duration around it, at which point ambient sound becomes content. The essays in Silence (1961) make the method explicit — that the composer’s task can be to arrange the conditions of listening rather than to supply the material. Pauline Oliveros went further and built a discipline. Sonic Meditations6 (1971) is a set of text instructions, many performable by non-musicians, and the practice she named Deep Listening distinguishes hearing (involuntary, physiological) from listening (voluntary, trained, and capable of improvement over decades). Deep Listening: A Composer’s Sound Practice7 (2005) is unembarrassed about the timescale: she describes it as the work of a life.

Against this stands the economic argument, which is not new either. Herbert Simon set it out in 1971: “a wealth of information creates a poverty of attention.” The condition Simon predicted is now the operating environment of every design studio, and the industry’s response has been to treat attention as a resource to be defended with tooling — notification settings, focus modes, blocked calendars. This is attention as scarcity management. Weil, James, Murdoch, Cage and Oliveros all describe something else: attention as a capacity that grows under practice. Jenny Odell5’s How to Do Nothing (2019) is right that refusal is necessary and stops short of the harder claim, which is that the reclaimed time has to be spent on something structured or it simply fills back up.

So the position of this pillar is that a design studio should run a sitting practice the way it runs a type library — as infrastructure, maintained whether or not anyone is currently using it, with a fixed time and a real address. Josef Albers ran the nearest precedent: Interaction of Color1 (1963) is a course in which students are given no theory until they have looked, repeatedly, at the same colored papers and been wrong about them. Albers understood that seeing is a discipline with exercises. This pillar proposes that attention is the same, that the exercise is sitting, and that a studio which cannot spare an hour a week for it is making a claim about its priorities whether or not it says so out loud.

Applications

Research transformed into applications.

  • OP/EN — a weekly open sitting, free, no registration, open to anyone; held at the studio. Project
  • Dharma talks — short talks given alongside the sitting, recorded.
  • The salon at 1029 Lake Street — the room itself: a fixed address in Oak Park that hosts the sitting, the listening sessions and the gatherings.
  • Format: one hour. No fee, no membership, no app. The only fixed elements are the time, the address and the fact that it happens whether or not anybody comes.
