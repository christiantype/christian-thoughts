Archive and the Humanities

How does a working studio build an archive that changes what gets made, rather than one that only records what was made? Design has a short memory. A discipline that reinvents its own arguments every eight years is not a discipline, it is a fashion cycle — and the humanities exist precisely to lengthen the span over which a field can remember what it already knows.

Looking

Written while the Valicenti archive is being assembled.

The oral history is worth more than the artifacts, by a margin that surprised me. An hour with the person who made the thing recovers the client’s objection, the version that was killed, and the constraint that produced the solution — none of which survives in the printed piece. The corollary is uncomfortable for a design studio: the most valuable archival asset is a person’s time, it is the one asset that expires, and it is the one nobody budgets for.

Warburg8’s method transferred better than expected. Arranging work by gesture rather than by date or client surfaced continuities across thirty years that a chronology hides entirely, and two of those juxtapositions have already changed a current project. That is the citation test passing, at a small scale.

What did not work: the first eighteen months of this were effectively digitisation, on the assumption that getting everything scanned was the prerequisite. It was not. A complete scan of material nobody has described is a pile with better lighting — Derrida2’s point about the archiving structure determining the archivable content, learned the slow way. Description is the work; scanning is logistics.

Also unresolved: access. An archive that only the studio can use fails its own standard, and every option for opening it — a site, a reading room, a deposit with an institution — carries an obligation the studio cannot currently fund. Saying that the archive is for everyone while it sits on a drive in Oak Park is the exact failure Benjamin1 describes, and naming it is not the same as fixing it.

Thinking

This is where we test our thinking.

Jacques Derrida’s Archive Fever (1995; trans. Eric Prenowitz, 1996) contains the sentence that ought to be pinned above every digitisation project: “the technical structure of the archiving archive also determines the structure of the archivable content even in its very coming into existence and in its relationship to the future.” The medium of storage is not neutral to what gets stored. A shelf produces one kind of memory; a searchable database produces another; an Instagram grid produces a third, and the third is the one most design studios are actually using. Derrida’s other move is to recover the word’s origin — arkheion, the magistrate’s house, where documents were kept by those with the authority to interpret them. An archive is a claim to authority before it is a service to scholarship, and pretending otherwise is the first mistake.

Michel Foucault had already made the structural version of the argument. In The Archaeology of Knowledge5 (1969) the archive is not a collection of documents at all but “the system that governs the appearance of statements as unique events” — the set of rules determining what can be said, what counts as a contribution, what is audible as an argument in a given period. Read that way, design’s amnesia is not an accident of poor record-keeping. It is produced by the rules of the field: a discipline whose public channel is the portfolio, and whose portfolio shows outcomes rather than reasoning, is structurally incapable of accumulating knowledge. It can only accumulate images.

Aby Warburg’s response to a related problem remains the most useful precedent available. The Mnemosyne Atlas (1924–29, unfinished at his death) abandoned chronology and category and instead pinned photographs to black cloth panels, arranging them by gesture, tension and what Warburg called Pathosformel — formulas of emotion that recur across centuries and media. Ernst Gombrich6’s biography (1970) makes clear how deliberate the method was: Warburg’s library was famously shelved by the “law of the good neighbor,” on the principle that the book you need is rarely the one you were looking for but the one beside it. The Atlas is an argument that juxtaposition is itself a scholarly instrument — that placing two images side by side can constitute a claim. This is a designer’s competence, and design has almost never used it on its own history.

Walter Benjamin supplies the counterweight to the idea that any of this is neutral. “Unpacking My Library” (1931) is an essay about the disorder of a real collection, about how the objects carry the biography of the collector rather than a map of the subject. His “Theses on the Philosophy of History” (1940) go further, insisting that the documents a culture preserves are the documents of its victors, and that the historian’s job is to brush history against the grain. Both are correctives to the fantasy of the complete archive. A studio archive assembled from what happens to have survived — what was billed, photographed, printed and kept — is a record of what the studio was paid to do, not of what it thought.

The contemporary version of the problem is described precisely by Hal Foster in “An Archival Impulse” (October4 110, 2004). Foster identifies a generation of artists working with found documents and observes that their archives are “found yet constructed, factual yet fictive, public yet private” — and, crucially, that the impulse is not nostalgic but generative: the material is dug up in order to make something new possible. That is the standard this pillar is trying to meet. Wolfgang Ernst, in Digital Memory and the Archive3 (2013), adds the technical caution — that a digital archive is not a place where things rest but a process that must be continually re-executed, and that anything not actively migrated is not preserved but merely postponed.

Against all of this sits the state of design’s own historiography. Philip Meggs’s A History of Graphic Design7 (1983) was a genuine achievement and also a single narrative, assembled largely from printed artifacts by one author, and for two decades it was effectively the field’s memory. The problem is not that Meggs was wrong; it is that a discipline with one history has no historiography — no argument between accounts, no primary sources a student can go back to and read differently. Rick Valicenti’s studio has been producing work continuously since 1981, most of it documented only as images, with the reasoning held in the memory of the people who were in the room. When that generation stops working, the reasoning goes with it. That is the specific, local, urgent version of the general problem, and it is the reason this pillar exists.

So the position is that a design studio should run an archive the way a humanities department runs one: with primary sources, with the people who made the work recorded speaking about it, with juxtaposition treated as argument, and with the whole thing built to be used by people who are not the studio. The measure of success is not completeness. It is whether anyone ever changes their mind because of it.
